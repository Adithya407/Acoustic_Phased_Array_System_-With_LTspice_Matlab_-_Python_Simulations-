RP2040 true-time-delay beamformer

Steering sound with time delays

How six small speakers form one beam, why a linear ramp of delays points it, and how the spacing, sampling rate and listener distance decide what you actually hear. Every result is derived from first principles and backed by an interactive model.

  • N = 6 speakers
  • face 24 × 20 mm, a = 20 mm
  • spacing d = 50 mm
  • steering up to ±45°
  • fs 96 kHz
  • DAC AD5628
  • c = 343 m/s

01Waves and phase

Everything in this project comes from adding waves with different phases.

A small speaker radiates approximately as a point source. At distance \(r\) its sound pressure is a spherical wave:

\[ p(r,t)=\frac{A}{r}\,e^{\,j(\omega t-kr)},\qquad \omega=2\pi f,\quad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}=\frac{\omega}{c},\quad \lambda=\frac{c}{f} \]
SymbolMeaningValue in air
\(c\)speed of sound343 m/s at about 20 °C
\(f\), \(\omega\)frequency, angular frequency20 Hz to 20 kHz
\(\lambda\)wavelength17 m at 20 Hz down to 1.7 cm at 20 kHz
\(k\)wavenumber: phase gained per metrerad/m

The term \(kr\) is the phase the wave has built up after travelling \(r\). Waves that meet in phase add (constructive interference); waves that meet half a cycle apart cancel. The pressure from many sources is just the sum of the individual pressures (superposition).

02The diffraction grating

An array of speakers is mathematically the same object as an optical grating.

A diffraction grating is a plate with many identical, equally spaced slits (period \(d\)). A plane wave falling on it makes every slit act as a new source (the Huygens–Fresnel principle). All slits are driven by the same wave, so they differ only in position. On a distant screen you see their interference.

The exact distance R(x) and the far-field approximation

Put the array on the x-axis with its centre at the origin. A source sits at \((x,0)\) and the listener at distance \(r\), angle \(\theta\) from broadside, i.e. at \((r\sin\theta,\ r\cos\theta)\). The exact source-to-listener distance is

\[ R(x)=\sqrt{(r\sin\theta-x)^2+(r\cos\theta)^2}=\sqrt{r^2-2rx\sin\theta+x^2}. \]

Write \(R=r\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\) with \(\varepsilon=-2(x/r)\sin\theta+(x/r)^2\) and use \(\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\approx 1+\varepsilon/2-\varepsilon^2/8\):

\[ R(x)\approx \underbrace{r}_{\text{common}}\;-\;\underbrace{x\sin\theta}_{\text{parallel rays}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{x^2\cos^2\theta}{2r}}_{\text{curvature, dropped}} \]

In the far field the curvature term is negligible. In the amplitude \(1/R\) every source can be replaced by \(1/r\). In the phase \(kR\) the linear term must be kept, because \(k\,x\sin\theta\) is a sizeable fraction of \(2\pi\). That term creates the interference pattern.

Summing the sources: the array factor

With sources at \(x_n=nd\), \(n=0\ldots N-1\):

\[ p\approx\frac{A}{r}e^{j(\omega t-kr)}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{jn\psi},\qquad \psi=kd\sin\theta \]

The sum is a geometric series:

\[ \mathrm{AF}(\theta)=\frac{1-e^{jN\psi}}{1-e^{j\psi}},\qquad |\mathrm{AF}|=\left|\frac{\sin(N\psi/2)}{\sin(\psi/2)}\right|,\qquad I\propto|\mathrm{AF}|^2 \]
FeatureConditionMeaning
Principal maxima\(\psi=2\pi m\Rightarrow d\sin\theta=m\lambda\)The grating equation: all \(N\) waves arrive in phase, \(|\mathrm{AF}|=N\)
Nulls\(N\psi/2=p\pi\), \(p\) not a multiple of \(N\)The \(N\) phasors close into a polygon and cancel

If the plane wave arrives tilted at \(\theta_i\), each slit is driven a little later than its neighbour and the grating equation becomes \(d(\sin\theta-\sin\theta_i)=m\lambda\). The zero-order beam leaves at \(\theta=\theta_i\). A speaker array reproduces this tilt electronically with time delays.

03Full far-field wave function

Single-speaker envelope × array factor, and how a phase step turns into a time delay.

Each speaker is modelled as a flat strip of width \(a\). Integrating the far-field phase across its face gives the single-slit pattern:

\[ \int_{-a/2}^{a/2}e^{jkx\sin\theta}\,dx=a\,\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta\right)}{\frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta} \]

Adding a phase step \(\Phi\) between neighbouring speakers makes the array-factor phase \(\psi=\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta+\Phi\). The total wave function is the product (pattern multiplication):

\[ \Psi=\Psi_0\,\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta\right)}{\frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta}\;\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta+\Phi\right)\right)}{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta+\frac{\Phi}{2}\right)},\qquad I=|\Psi|^2 \]

\(\Psi_0\) contains the spherical wave \(e^{j(\omega t-kr)}/r\) and a common phase factor that disappears when the magnitude is taken. The main beam is where the denominator goes to zero:

\[ \frac{\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta+\frac{\Phi}{2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\theta=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-\lambda\Phi}{2\pi d}\right)=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-c}{2\pi d}\frac{\Phi}{f}\right) \]

A delay \(t_d\) multiplies a tone by \(e^{-j\omega t_d}\), so \(\Phi=-2\pi f\,t_d\) and

\[ \theta=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{c\,t_d}{d}\right)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad t_d=\frac{d\sin\theta}{c} \]

The frequency has cancelled. A fixed time step \(t_d\) supplies exactly the right phase \(\Phi=-2\pi f t_d\) at every frequency at once. A fixed phase \(\Phi\) does not.

Three things the short derivation hides:

  • Other zeros of the denominator. It also vanishes at \(\psi/2=m\pi\), giving \(\sin\theta=c\,t_d/d+m\lambda/d\). Those are the grating lobes (section 7).
  • The envelope does not steer. \(\Phi\) appears only in the array factor. A steered beam is scaled by \(\mathrm{sinc}^2(\pi a\sin\theta_0/\lambda)\). For \(a=20\) mm this costs less than 1.5 dB below 11 kHz.
  • Range of validity. \(|c\,t_d/d|\le1\), so the largest useful step is \(d/c\) (steering to ±90°). A round cone gives \(2J_1(x)/x\) instead of the sinc: the same broad bump with slightly different nulls.

04From grating to time delays

The time-domain derivation, the delay for each speaker, and how the firmware applies it.

Speaker \(n\) plays \(s(t-\tau_n)\). Its sound reaches a distant listener in direction \(\theta\) at

\[ t_n(\theta)=\tau_n+\frac{R_n}{c}=\tau_n+\frac{r}{c}-\frac{x_n\sin\theta}{c}. \]

For the loudest possible sound at \(\theta_0\), every copy must arrive at the same instant, for any waveform:

\[ \tau_n-\frac{x_n\sin\theta_0}{c}=\text{const}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \tau_n=\frac{x_n\sin\theta_0}{c}\;\;\xrightarrow{x_n=nd}\;\; \tau_n=n\,t_d,\quad t_d=\frac{d\sin\theta_0}{c} \]

This is the grating equation with \(c\,t_d\) playing the role of \(d\sin\theta_i\): the delay ramp acts like light falling on the grating at an angle, created electronically.

The delay for each speaker

The delays are not all equal. The step between neighbours, \(t_d\), is the same everywhere; each speaker's delay grows linearly across the array. A delay common to all speakers only shifts the output in time and does not steer. Number the speakers \(n=0\ldots5\) from left to right, take \(\theta\) positive to the right (seen from behind the array), and shift so the smallest delay is zero, since a delay line cannot advance a signal:

\[ \tau_n=n\,t_d-\min_n(n\,t_d),\qquad t_d=\frac{d\sin\theta_0}{c}\ \ (\text{signed}) \]

The firmware keeps one circular buffer and gives each channel its own read offset. The largest delay (±90°) is \(5d/c=729\ \mu s=70\) samples, so a 128-sample buffer covers every angle.

#define BUF_LEN 128                 // power of two, larger than the 70-sample maximum
int16_t buf[BUF_LEN];
uint32_t w = 0;                     // write index
uint8_t D[6];                       // per-speaker delay in samples, e.g. {0,7,14,21,28,35}

// every sample period (96 kHz)
buf[w & (BUF_LEN - 1)] = input_sample;
for (int n = 0; n < 6; n++)
    dac_out[n] = buf[(w - D[n]) & (BUF_LEN - 1)];
w++;
// write the six values to the AD5628, then pulse LDAC so they update together

05Time delay vs phase shift

Why a phase-only steerer's beam moves with frequency.

A phase-only steerer applies the step \(\alpha=k_0d\sin\theta_0\), computed at a design frequency \(f_0\), at every frequency. In the frequency domain the true-time-delay array factor is

\[ \mathrm{AF}=\sum_n e^{jn(kd\sin\theta-\omega t_d)},\qquad kd\sin\theta-\omega t_d=\frac{\omega}{c}\left(d\sin\theta-c\,t_d\right) \]

which peaks at \(\sin\theta=c\,t_d/d\) for every \(\omega\). The phase-only version peaks where \(kd\sin\theta=k_0d\sin\theta_0\):

\[ \sin\theta_{\text{actual}}=\frac{f_0}{f}\sin\theta_0 \]

The beam direction depends on frequency. This effect is called beam squint, and it is the same dispersion that makes an optical grating split white light into colours. Example with \(f_0=2\) kHz, \(\theta_0=30°\):

07Grating lobes, f_max and f_min

Where the extra beams appear, and the frequency band in which the array steers cleanly.

Grating-lobe angles

All speakers add fully whenever the path difference minus the applied delay is a whole number of wavelengths. For a steady tone, one cycle late looks exactly like on time:

\[ d\sin\theta-d\sin\theta_0=m\lambda\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_m=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\sin\theta_0+m\frac{\lambda}{d}\right),\quad |\sin\theta_0+m\lambda/d|\le1 \]

\(m=0\) is the main beam; each other real solution is a grating lobe. The array factor is at full strength at every such peak, so only the single-speaker envelope sets its level relative to the main beam:

\[ L_m=10\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{sinc}^2(a\sin\theta_m/\lambda)}{\mathrm{sinc}^2(a\sin\theta_0/\lambda)},\qquad \mathrm{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin\pi u}{\pi u} \]
f (d = 50 mm, θ₀ = +30°)λ/dm = −1m = −2Grating lobes

A steer to the right brings grating lobes in from the left edge (m = −1 first), and they sweep towards the main beam as frequency rises. With time delays the main beam stays fixed at every frequency, but every grating lobe still moves with frequency.

f_max: keep every grating lobe outside the visible window

Only \(|\sin\theta|\le1\) corresponds to real directions. For a steer to \(+\theta_0\) the nearest lobe is \(m=-1\) at \(\sin\theta_0-\lambda/d\). It stays invisible if

\[ \sin\theta_0-\frac{\lambda}{d}\lt-1\iff d\lt\frac{\lambda}{1+|\sin\theta_0|}\iff f\lt f_{\max}=\frac{c}{d\,(1+|\sin\theta_{\max}|)} \]

A lobe has width, and its skirt enters before its peak. The half-power point of \(\sin(Nx)/(N\sin x)\) is at \(Nx\approx1.392\), i.e. \(\Delta(\sin\theta)_{1/2}=0.443\lambda/(Nd)\). Keeping that edge outside as well:

\[ f_{\max}=\frac{c\,(1-0.443/N)}{d\,(1+|\sin\theta_{\max}|)} \]

f_min: the array must be about one wavelength long

A beam exists only if the pattern falls to a null somewhere. The first broadside null is at \(N\psi/2=\pi\), i.e. \(\sin\theta_{\text{null}}=\lambda/(Nd)\). It lies inside the window when

\[ \frac{\lambda}{Nd}\le1\iff f\ge f_{\min}=\frac{c}{Nd} \]

At \(f_{\min}\) the beam is still broad (about 53°). When steered, the null on the endfire side sits at \(\sin\theta_0+\lambda/(Nd)\) and appears only above \(c/(Nd(1-\sin\theta_0))\), which is twice \(f_{\min}\) at 30° and 3.4 times at 45°.

The band ratio is set by N alone

\[ \frac{f_{\max}}{f_{\min}}=\frac{N-0.443}{1+\sin\theta_{\max}}\qquad(\text{simple form: }N/(1+\sin\theta_{\max})) \]

The spacing only moves the band up or down; the number of speakers sets how wide it is. The narrowest clean beam, at the top of the band, is about \(0.886(1+\sin\theta_{\max})/N\) rad and also depends only on N.

08Choosing the spacing d

Why the 20 mm speakers at 50 mm spacing were chosen for music with most of its energy between 200 Hz and 4 kHz.

The smallest possible d is the speaker width (speakers touching). A smaller d always pushes grating lobes higher, but it also shortens the array and raises \(f_{\min}\). For music, the best choice is the largest d that still keeps grating lobes out up to 4 kHz, which moves the band as low as possible:

\[ d^\ast=\frac{c\,(1-0.443/N)}{4000\,(1+\sin\theta_{\max})}\qquad N=6:\ \ 53\ \text{mm at }\pm30°,\ \ 46.5\ \text{mm at }\pm45° \]

Two other options were considered. The 66 mm speakers touching (d = 66 mm) steer about 0.87–3.2 kHz at ±30°: lower, but the top of that band is short of 4 kHz. 09Far-field distance

Where 2L²/λ comes from, and what it means for a listener.

The far-field formula drops the term \(x^2\cos^2\theta/(2r)\) from \(R(x)\). It is largest for the end speakers (\(x=\pm L/2\)) at broadside:

\[ \delta_{\max}=\frac{L^2}{8r},\qquad \Delta\phi=k\,\delta_{\max}=\frac{\pi L^2}{4\lambda r}\le\frac{\pi}{8}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_{\text{ff}}\ge\frac{2L^2}{\lambda} \]

\(L=(N-1)d\) is the distance between the end speakers' centres (0.25 m here); adding one speaker width gives 0.27 m and a slightly larger, more cautious distance. The π/8 limit is the antenna-measurement convention. A looser π/4 gives \(L^2/\lambda\), which is enough for the main beam only.

Beyond about 2L (0.5 m) a listener already hears the beam pointing the right way. From 2L²/λ onwards the shape, including nulls and side lobes, is final. 11Sampling and hardware

Why 96 kHz, and what the AD5628 and RP2040 allow.

Delays can only be whole sample periods \(T_s=1/f_s\), a distance step of \(cT_s\). Rounding the step \(t_d\) limits the angles to \(\sin\theta=m\,cT_s/d\). Rounding each speaker's delay separately keeps the average angle, but leaves timing errors of up to ±\(T_s/2\), a phase error of ±\(\pi f/f_s\) that grows with frequency and raises the side lobes.

Update all channels together with LDAC (or the software LDAC command). Without it, each channel updates one frame (about 0.72 µs) after the previous one, which adds an unwanted delay ramp. The RP2040 hardware SPI sends at most 16 bits per frame, so a PIO state machine sending 32-bit frames is the simpler route. The -2 variant (2.5 V reference, 5 V full scale) needs VDD ≈ 5 V; check its logic-high input threshold against the RP2040's 3.3 V outputs. Keep 44.1 / 48 kHz selectable in firmware so quantization can be measured as a variable.
